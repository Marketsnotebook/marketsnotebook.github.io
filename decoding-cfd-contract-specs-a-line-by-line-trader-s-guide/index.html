Decoding CFD Contract Specs: A Line-by-Line Trader’s Guide

September 30, 2026 · Markets Notebook

A CFD contract specification appears on your platform as a list of numbers and abbreviations. These figures dictate the capital required, the profit generated by price moves, and the costs incurred for overnight positions. Misinterpreting any line can turn a planned trade into an unexpected loss. The following sections break down each element of a typical CFD specification, demonstrate how numbers are calculated, and illustrate their practical impact on a retail trader’s account.

Contract Size: The Foundation of Your Trade

The contract size is the base quantity of the underlying asset that a single CFD represents. It acts as a multiplier, converting a price quote into a monetary value.

  • Forex pairs: Most brokers define one standard lot as 100,000 units of the base currency. A mini lot is typically 10,000 units, and a micro lot is 1,000 units.
  • Indices: Contracts are often quoted in “points.” A common size is 1 point per contract, but many brokers use 10 points per contract for a larger notional value.
  • Commodities: Size varies by product. For instance, a gold CFD might represent 1 troy ounce, while a crude oil CFD could represent 1 barrel.

The contract size determines the trade’s notional value, calculated by multiplying the size and the underlying’s current price.

Worked example – forex vs index

Instrument Contract size Current price Notional value (per contract)
EUR/USD 100,000 units 1.0850 USD 100,000 × 1.0850 = 108,500 USD
S&P 500 1 point 4,300 points 1 × 4,300 = 4,300 USD (if broker defines $1 per point)
S&P 500 (10-point) 10 points 4,300 points 10 × 4,300 = 43,000 USD

A trader assuming a “lot” always means 100,000 units may miscalculate position size if the broker uses a 10-point index contract. Always verify the contract size before determining risk or position size.

Tick Size and Tick Value: Measuring Price Movement

Two related fields describe the granularity of price changes and the monetary impact of each movement.

Tick Size

The smallest increment by which a price can move. In forex, a tick is typically 0.00001 (one pip for most major pairs). For an index, it may be 0.1 point. Some brokers offer finer ticks (e.g., 0.01 point) for highly liquid instruments.

Tick Value

The profit or loss realized from a one-tick move on a standard contract. It is calculated as:

tick value = tick size × contract size

For forex, the tick size is a pip, and the tick value becomes the pip value. For indices, the tick value is the monetary amount associated with a one-point move.

Worked example – EUR/USD

  • Contract size: 100,000 units (1 standard lot)
  • Tick size: 0.00001 (1 pip)
  • Tick value = 0.00001 × 100,000 = 1 USD per pip

If the market moves 5 pips (50 ticks) in your favor, the profit is:

profit = tick value × number of ticks = 1 USD × 50 = 50 USD

For a crude oil CFD where each tick is 0.01 USD and the contract size is 1 barrel:

tick value = 0.01 × 1 = 0.01 USD per tick

A 10-tick move (0.10 USD) yields 0.10 USD profit per contract. This highlights how contract size and tick size together dictate the scale of any profit or loss.

Leverage and Margin: Understanding Borrowed Capital

Leverage allows a trader to control a larger notional exposure than the cash deposited. Brokers express this relationship in two ways: margin percentage and leverage ratio.

  • Margin %: The proportion of the notional value that must be held as collateral. A 1% margin means you must allocate 1% of the trade’s notional value from your account.
  • Leverage ratio: The inverse of the margin percentage. 1% margin equals 100:1 leverage; 5% margin equals 20:1 leverage.

Higher leverage amplifies both gains and losses. The margin requirement can differ between instrument classes (e.g., 2% for major forex pairs, 5% for commodity CFDs) and may be reduced during periods of low volatility.

Margin Call

A margin call occurs when the account equity falls below the required margin for all open positions. Brokers typically issue an automated warning and may start closing positions if the shortfall is not addressed.

Worked example – 1% margin on EUR/USD

  • Contract size: 100,000 units
  • Current price: 1.0800 USD
  • Notional value = 100,000 × 1.0800 = 108,000 USD
  • Required margin = 1% × 108,000 = 1,080 USD

If a trader opens a position with precisely 1,080 USD in the account, any adverse price movement that reduces equity below that amount (including commissions and swap charges) will trigger a margin call. Maintaining a buffer, often 20-30% above the required margin, is a practical risk-management strategy.

Swap Rates: The Cost of Holding Overnight

A swap (or rollover) is the interest adjustment applied when a position is held past the end of the trading day. Contract specifications list two values:

  • Swap Long: Credit or debit applied to a long (buy) position.
  • Swap Short: Credit or debit applied to a short (sell) position.

For forex CFDs, the swap reflects the interest rate differential between the two currencies, adjusted for the broker’s financing charge. For indices and commodities, the swap is based on the cost of financing the underlying exposure (e.g., dividend yields for equity indices, storage costs for metals).

Swaps can be positive (a credit) or negative (a debit). The magnitude is usually expressed in the account currency per lot, per night. For example, a swap of –0.5 USD for a standard EUR/USD lot means the trader pays 50 cents each night the position remains open.

Swap rates are dynamic:

  • Central bank rate changes, such as a Fed rate hike, instantly alter the interest differential and the corresponding swap for USD-linked pairs.
  • Market holidays can shift the number of days a swap is applied, leading to “triple swap” days.

Because swaps are applied daily, a small negative swap can erode profits on a multi-day trend trade. Conversely, a positive swap can provide a small but predictable income stream for a carry-trade strategy.

Trading Hours: When Can You Trade?

Each CFD specifies its active trading window in a particular timezone (commonly GMT or the broker’s server time). The window includes:

  • Start time: When the first price quote becomes available for the day.
  • End time: When the last quote is generated before the market is considered closed.

For forex, many brokers mirror the interbank market, offering near-continuous trading from 00:00 GMT to 23:59 GMT, with brief pauses for liquidity. For indices, trading hours follow the exchange listing the underlying asset (e.g., the S&P 500 follows NYSE hours, 14:30–21:00 GMT). Commodities may have split sessions (e.g., oil contracts trade from 20:00 GMT to 02:00 GMT and 09:00–20:00 GMT).

Common mistake

Retail traders often assume all CFDs trade 24 hours a day because their platform shows continuous quotes. In reality, prices may be stale or synthetic outside official sessions, and spreads can widen significantly. Executing a market order during low-liquidity windows can result in slippage or execution at a price far from the displayed level.

Triple Swap Day: The Weekend Interest Charge

CFD swaps are calculated daily, but the market is closed over the weekend. Brokers reconcile the three days (Saturday, Sunday, and Monday morning) with a single “triple swap” charge, typically applied on Wednesday night (or the broker’s designated swap day).

  • Why Wednesday? By applying the triple swap mid-week, the broker distributes the weekend cost across the trading week, avoiding a sharp increase on Monday when many traders open new positions.

If a trader’s standard overnight swap is –0.5 USD per lot, the triple swap will be –1.5 USD for that night. For a carry-trade relying on a positive swap, the triple-swap day can convert a modest credit into a noticeable debit.

Brokers often publish the exact time (e.g., 23:59 GMT) when the triple swap is debited. Knowing this time helps a trader avoid opening a new position that immediately incurs the extra charge or, alternatively, closing a position before the charge is applied to retain expected carry.

Key Takeaways

  • Contract specifications define the size, price granularity, and financing costs of every CFD, directly impacting risk, profit potential, and margin requirements.
  • Leverage, expressed as margin %, amplifies both gains and losses. Maintaining a buffer above the required margin reduces the likelihood of a margin call.
  • Swap rates are a daily cost or credit for holding positions overnight. They fluctuate with interest rate environments and can reduce profitability on multi-day trades.
  • Verify the trading hours for each instrument and be aware of the broker’s triple-swap day to avoid unexpected charges or execution issues.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important spec to check?

All specifications are important, but Contract Size and Margin % have the most immediate impact on trade sizing and risk exposure. Contract size determines the monetary value of each price movement, while margin % dictates the capital required to open the trade.

Can swap rates change?

Yes. Swap rates are tied to underlying financing costs—interbank rates for forex or dividend/financing yields for indices and commodities. Central bank policy changes, benchmark rate adjustments, and seasonal dividend changes can cause daily variations in published swap rates.

What’s the difference between Tick Size and Pip?

Tick Size is the smallest price increment an instrument can move, as defined by the broker (e.g., 0.00001 for EUR/USD). A pip is a conventional unit of price change used by traders; for most major forex pairs, a pip equals 10 ticks (0.00010). For index CFDs, a pip may be defined as one full point, which could correspond to a single tick if the broker’s tick size is 1.0. Understanding both ensures accurate calculation of tick value and pip value.

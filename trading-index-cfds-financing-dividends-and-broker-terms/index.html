Trading index CFDs: financing, dividends and broker terms

November 6, 2026 · Markets Notebook

An index CFD may look inexpensive to enter but cost more to hold than its spread suggests. Overnight financing, dividend adjustments, contract type and trading hours all affect the cost and risks of keeping a position open.

How overnight financing changes the cost of a cash index CFD

Cash index CFDs are generally designed to track a cash index level and may incur overnight financing when held beyond the broker’s daily cut-off. The charge reflects the cost of maintaining exposure, but its calculation and application depend on the broker and contract. A position closed before the cut-off may be treated differently from one held through it, so check the relevant time zone and exact charge time.

Check the stated financing rate, its calculation basis and whether it can change. An annualised rate is not the same as a daily charge: a broker may convert it using a day-count basis such as 365 days, and the applicable rate may include a broker markup. Ask whether the charge is calculated on the position’s notional value or another base, and whether weekend financing is applied on a particular weekday or spread across several days.

For example, a $10,000 position charged at 5% annualised for one day would cost about $1.37 before any markup or other fees: $10,000 × 5% ÷ 365. Actual charges can differ if the broker uses another basis, applies a different rate or calculates the position value differently. Focusing on a small spread difference while ignoring several weeks of financing can be misleading; the financing may outweigh the entry saving.

What index dividend adjustments mean for long and short positions

When constituents of an index go ex-dividend, the cash index may be adjusted downward to reflect those distributions. CFD providers commonly account for this through a cash adjustment to the trading account or an equivalent product-price adjustment. The exact mechanism and timing are set by the broker’s contract terms; adjustments may be made around the relevant ex-dividend date.

A long position may receive a credit and a short position may incur a debit, but the amount, timing and presentation vary by broker and product. Treatment can also depend on the account and jurisdiction. Check whether the adjustment appears as a separate transaction, how the provider calculates it, and whether its tax treatment differs from that of a direct dividend payment.

In a simplified example, a $2-per-index-point adjustment on a position worth $10 per point would mean a $20 adjustment. This illustrates the calculation; it does not predict what any specific broker will apply. A dividend credit is not free income: the index price may also fall by approximately the dividend amount, while spreads and financing continue to affect the position.

Cash and futures index CFDs can have different quoted prices

Cash index CFDs typically reference a cash index level. Futures CFDs track a futures contract, whose price can reflect the time to expiry, interest rates and expected dividends. As a result, the two products can show different levels for what traders informally call “the same index,” without either quote necessarily being wrong.

The difference between a futures price and the cash index is known as the basis. It can change with market conditions and as the futures contract approaches expiry. Futures-based products may also be rolled from one contract to another; the broker’s method and any associated adjustment are set out in the contract terms.

Compare the reference market, expiry or rollover method, spread and holding costs, not just the displayed index level. Cash and futures CFDs may have different prices and cost structures, even when they provide exposure to the same broad market.

Check trading hours, daily breaks and holiday schedules

Index CFD hours vary by instrument and broker. Some products follow local exchange hours, while others offer extended sessions. Check the platform’s stated time zone, daily maintenance breaks, weekend availability and holiday schedule before planning entries, exits or stop orders. The displayed market time may differ from the time zone used for the broker’s financing cut-off.

Outside the underlying exchange’s main session, liquidity may be thinner and spreads may widen. Prices can also move in response to news or activity in related markets, and the broker’s quote may not behave exactly like the index during its primary trading session. These conditions can increase the chance that a stop order is filled at a different price from its trigger, especially if the market moves quickly.

Confirm whether pending orders remain active during a closure and how the broker handles a gap when trading resumes. During a gap, a stop order generally does not guarantee a fill at its trigger price unless the product or order type provides a specific guarantee under stated conditions. Check holiday hours in advance; normal weekday sessions may not apply.

Compare broker terms on a like-for-like basis

For each index, record the contract size, value per point, minimum trade, margin requirement, spread and any commission. Then compare financing rates and cut-off times, dividend-adjustment rules, trading hours, price source and futures rollover terms. A broker’s margin requirement affects the cash needed to open a position, but does not cap potential losses.

To compare index CFD broker terms through an independent comparison resource, use the same instrument and position size at each provider. Confirm that the contracts are comparable: one may reference a cash index while another tracks a futures contract. Check whether spreads are fixed or variable, and whether a published typical spread applies only during a particular session.

Use a consistent test position and holding period—for example, one contract held for five nights—and estimate costs from each broker’s published terms. Record the spread when you expect to trade, any commission, each scheduled overnight charge, and any relevant dividend or rollover adjustment. If a cost is unclear in the broker’s terms, ask how it is calculated before relying on an estimate.

Estimate total holding cost before placing a trade

A useful estimate adds the expected spread and commission to financing, then accounts for dividend adjustments and any futures rollover or expiry effects. For a cash index CFD, a simple financing estimate might use position value × annualised rate ÷ the broker’s day-count basis for each chargeable day. This is only a starting point: use the broker’s actual schedule and confirm whether the rate and calculation base can change.

For a five-night comparison, list the charge expected on each night rather than multiplying an annualised rate by five without checking the day-count basis or weekend schedule. For instance, if the broker publishes a daily charge of 1.40 in the account currency for the position, five identical chargeable nights would total 7.00. If one charge covers multiple days or rates vary, use the published daily schedule instead. Add estimated entry and exit spread costs and any commission for a more useful total.

Stress-test the estimate for a wider spread, a changed financing rate and a dividend date during the planned holding period. Also consider price risk: index CFDs are leveraged, and a gap can cause a loss larger than the initial margin. Include margin and potential gap losses in the decision, not just expected fees. A low estimated holding cost does not make a position low-risk if the market can move sharply while it is open.

Key takeaways

  • Compare total costs over your expected holding period, not spreads in isolation.
  • Read the broker’s specific financing and dividend-adjustment rules for each index CFD.
  • Identify whether the product tracks a cash index or a futures contract before comparing prices.
  • Confirm trading hours, breaks and holiday schedules because liquidity and spreads can change outside the main session.

Frequently asked questions

Do all index CFDs charge overnight financing?

No. Cash CFDs commonly have overnight financing, while futures-based products may reflect costs in their price and rollover terms. Check the specific contract.

Will I always receive a dividend adjustment on a long index CFD?

Not necessarily in the same amount or form across brokers. Check the product terms, relevant ex-dividend date and how adjustments are applied.

Why do two brokers show different prices for the same index?

They may offer different contract types, reference prices, trading sessions or spreads; cash and futures prices can also differ because of the futures basis.

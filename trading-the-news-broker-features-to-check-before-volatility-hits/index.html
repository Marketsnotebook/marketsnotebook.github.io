Trading the News: Broker Features to Check Before Volatility Hits

November 16, 2026 · Markets Notebook

Learn how execution models, spread history, slippage terms, distance limits and pending-order rules affect forex and CFD trades around scheduled releases.

A scheduled release can move a market faster than a platform updates its quote, widening spreads and making an intended entry or exit harder to achieve. Before trading, check not only the headline spread but also how your broker handles orders, stop prices and restrictions when liquidity thins.

Start with execution type, not the marketing label

Ask whether orders are handled on an agency or STP-style basis, through a dealing desk or under another arrangement. These labels alone do not tell you how a particular order will be handled: firms may use different arrangements for different products or accounts. Read the broker’s order-execution policy and client agreement to understand whether it may act as counterparty and how it selects or routes prices.

Look for practical details. Does the broker source prices from one or more liquidity providers? When may an order be rejected or requoted? Is execution generally at the available market price, or can the broker apply price bounds? Check which legal entity holds your account and which regulator, if any, supervises it. Regulatory status and the entity’s terms matter more than a trading-style label when you need to understand complaint procedures and contractual protections.

Demo testing can show how an order ticket works, but offers weak evidence of live release execution. Demo accounts may not reflect live liquidity, latency, rejection rates or slippage. If possible, compare small live orders in ordinary conditions with the broker’s written policy, while recognising that calm-market results do not predict what will happen during a release.

A common mistake is to treat “no dealing desk” or “market execution” as a promise of a tight spread or a fill at the displayed price. Market execution generally means an order is filled at an available price, which may differ from the quote you saw. The policy, account terms and observed performance are the relevant evidence.

Measure spread widening around actual releases

Collect bid and ask observations for the instruments you intend to trade. A broker’s minimum spread is not a useful estimate of release-time costs unless it reflects the conditions and account you will actually use. Request dated spread data from the broker, or record quotes in the trading platform around releases such as consumer-price reports, employment data and central-bank decisions.

Compare matched time windows rather than isolated best or worst readings. For example, record spreads for the five minutes before and after each release, using the same instrument, account type, platform and time zone. Calculate the median to estimate a typical observation, and note the maximum to see how wide the spread became in your sample. One event does not represent all releases: liquidity and price movement vary across instruments and announcements.

Keep a record of the instrument, dates, time zone, platform, account and observation frequency. A screenshot shows what happened at one moment, but cannot establish a typical spread. When comparing brokers, use the same sampling method and distinguish quoted spread from any separate commission. For a commission-based account, estimate total transaction cost by adding spread cost and commission, converted to the same currency.

Worked example: Suppose a currency pair usually shows a spread of 0.8 pip but reaches 6 pips during your observed release window. The additional spread is 5.2 pips. If your position size makes each pip worth $10, the difference between those spread conditions is 5.2 × $10, or $52, for one entry. An exit can add further cost. Pip value depends on the instrument, position size and account currency, so calculate it for the trade rather than relying on a generic figure.

Read the slippage and execution policy closely

Find out how the broker handles market orders when prices move between submission and execution. An order may fill at a better or worse price than the quote shown when you clicked. Depending on the policy and platform, the broker may instead reject the order, requote it or apply a price tolerance. Check whether the rules differ by order type, instrument, account or market condition.

Read the stop-loss terms carefully. A stop price is generally a trigger, not a guaranteed execution price. If the market gaps through it or available liquidity is limited, a stop may fill at the next available price, which can be materially worse than the trigger. Ask whether the broker offers guaranteed stops for the relevant instrument and, if so, what conditions, availability limits or additional costs apply. An ordinary stop-loss does not necessarily have this feature.

Policies often describe exceptions for abnormal or fast markets. Identify what the broker means by those conditions and how it handles execution-quality complaints. Retain order confirmations, timestamps and platform records if a fill appears inconsistent with the stated policy. Ask about the process and time limit for raising a dispute, and keep the broker’s response.

A common mistake is to interpret a negative-slippage policy as a promise that every order will fill at its requested price, or that positive slippage will always be passed through. The policy should explain how price improvements and adverse movement are treated. Compare that language with actual order records, while remembering that a small sample cannot establish future execution.

Check minimum distance and order-change restrictions

For each instrument, verify the minimum distance between the market and an order or protective level. Platforms may display this as a stop level or distance rule, and the permitted distance can vary by instrument or account. Check whether the broker can change restrictions during volatile periods and how it notifies clients. A rule shown for a major currency pair may not apply to an index, commodity or individual share CFD.

Confirm which actions are affected. A distance rule may apply when placing or modifying a stop-loss, take-profit or pending order, not just when opening a position. Also check for a freeze level: if an order or position is too close to the market, the platform may prevent changes or cancellation. Find out whether these restrictions are fixed, published in the platform or imposed temporarily under specified conditions.

Worked example: If a broker requires a 10-pip minimum distance and the market is only 6 pips from your intended entry, a planned stop or pending order may be rejected or require adjustment. Moving a stop farther away changes the trade’s risk; it is not a harmless technical workaround. Recheck the permitted distance before the event, and do not assume the platform will accept a level just because it accepted a similar order earlier.

Know what happens to pending orders at a release

Ask which side of the quote triggers a stop-entry order. A buy order may be triggered by the ask and a sell order by the bid, but the broker’s instrument specification and platform rules are decisive. A spread that widens around a release can therefore trigger an order even if the price shown on a chart—which may display the bid or mid-price—appears not to have reached the trigger.

A trigger price is not necessarily a fill price. If the market gaps past a stop-entry level, the order may execute at the next available price, potentially well beyond the level you chose. For limit orders, check whether execution is at the requested price or better, and how partial fills or insufficient liquidity are handled. Confirm whether terms differ for CFDs and order types.

Check how one-sided triggers interact with OCO (one-cancels-the-other) instructions and attached stop-losses. In a fast move, both sides of an OCO order may be reached before cancellation takes effect, depending on the broker’s system and the market. Ask how attached protection is submitted and what happens if the entry fills only partially. Test order entry, modification and cancellation away from major events where possible; a pending order does not guarantee control over entry price or execution.

Compare broker claims with news-trading evidence

An independent resource such as Pro Forex Brokers’ guide to brokers for news trading can help you identify questions, but it does not replace checking current contract terms. Rankings and general descriptions may not match the entity, account type or instrument available to you. Use comparisons to identify what to investigate, not as proof that your order will receive a particular spread or fill.

Compare like with like. The same instrument, account type, order size, platform and release window provide more useful evidence than a general ranking. A standard account and a commission-based account can have different cost structures; a spread comparison alone may omit commissions. Ask each broker for dated spread or execution data, including the sampling period and any applicable conditions.

Keep the broker’s written response and distinguish an illustrative example from a binding service commitment. Terms may allow spreads or order restrictions to change, so historical observations do not guarantee future conditions. Recheck policies periodically, especially after account or platform changes, and save dated copies of relevant execution terms and instrument specifications.

Key takeaways

  • Evaluate execution policies and actual release-window spreads, not minimum-spread headlines.
  • A stop price or pending-order trigger does not guarantee the eventual fill price when markets gap or liquidity thins.
  • Check minimum-distance, freeze-level and order-modification rules for each instrument and account.
  • Use independent comparisons to guide questions, then verify current terms and conditions directly with the broker.

Frequently asked questions

Can a broker guarantee my order will fill at the displayed price during news?

Usually not for market or stop orders in fast conditions. Check the execution policy and assume the fill can differ from the quote or trigger price. If a broker offers a guaranteed stop for a specific instrument, check its terms, availability and cost; do not confuse it with an ordinary stop-loss.

Are fixed-spread accounts safer during major releases?

Not automatically. Check whether the spread is genuinely fixed, what exceptions apply, and whether execution, requotes or order restrictions change during volatility. A fixed spread does not by itself guarantee that an order will be accepted or filled at the displayed price.

How can I compare brokers’ spreads fairly?

Record the same instrument over the same release windows and compare typical and peak spreads, noting account type, platform, time zone and sample size. Include commissions where applicable, and use more than one release so a single unusual observation does not drive the comparison.

Majors, Minors and Exotics: Comparing Costs and Trading Behaviour

November 6, 2026 · Markets Notebook

A currency pair’s label can suggest how it trades, but it does not determine what a broker will charge or how far the price may move. To compare pairs fairly, assess spreads, commissions, volatility and trading hours alongside the position size and stop distance your strategy requires.

What makes a pair major, minor or exotic?

Major pairs combine the US dollar with another widely traded currency. Examples include EUR/USD and USD/JPY. These pairs are widely traded across global markets, though liquidity and costs can vary by broker and time of day.

Minors, also called crosses, pair major currencies without the US dollar. EUR/GBP and AUD/JPY are examples. Exotics typically combine a major currency with one from a smaller or less-traded market, such as USD/TRY or EUR/ZAR. These labels describe broad market categories, not fixed levels of risk or cost.

Classification is not fully standardized. Brokers may group pairs differently, and their available instruments can vary. Check the broker’s instrument list and contract specifications rather than assuming a pair is offered or categorized the same way everywhere.

Liquidity and spreads usually move together

More trading activity generally makes it easier to match buyers and sellers, supporting deeper liquidity and tighter bid-ask spreads. This is often true of liquid majors, particularly during overlapping European and US trading hours. But their spreads are not fixed: scheduled news, thin trading and changing market conditions can all affect quotes.

As an indicative comparison in normal markets, a liquid major might have a spread of about 0.1–1 pip, a cross about 0.5–3 pips, and an exotic several pips to tens of pips. These broad ranges are not promises. Actual spreads depend on the instrument, broker, account type and market conditions; they may widen substantially around events or when liquidity is thin.

Compare typical or time-weighted spreads, not advertised minimums. If an account charges commission, include it: a narrow spread alone does not make a trade cheaper. Also account for overnight financing and other charges relevant to your expected holding period.

Pips do not capture the full trading cost. A pip’s cash value depends on the pair, trade size and account currency. A one-pip move on two different pairs may represent different amounts in your account. Before comparing costs, calculate them in account currency for your intended position size.

Volatility and gaps can change the risk quickly

Volatility varies among pairs in every category. A major can move sharply after an interest-rate decision or significant economic release, while a usually quiet cross can accelerate after an unexpected policy change. The category alone cannot reliably predict how far a pair will move.

Exotics may be especially sensitive to local inflation, elections, central-bank actions or changes in capital controls. When liquidity thins, prices can move abruptly or gap between available quotes. A stop order helps define an exit plan, but its execution price is not guaranteed in a fast or gapping market.

Use a consistent measure to compare typical movement. Average true range (ATR), calculated over the same period for each pair, can help describe recent ranges. For example, comparing daily ATR over a set number of trading days is more informative than judging risk from the pair label. ATR describes past movement; it does not predict or guarantee the next move.

A common mistake is to set the same pip stop on every pair. A 20-pip stop may fall within one pair’s ordinary daily movement but be unusually wide for another. Stops should reflect the instrument’s movement, the trading setup and execution costs—not a universal pip distance.

Session timing affects execution and opportunity

EUR- and GBP-related pairs often see more activity during European hours, while USD pairs commonly see strong activity during the US session. Many major pairs are particularly active during the overlap between European and US hours. Greater activity can support tighter spreads, but does not prevent sudden widening around scheduled announcements.

Some emerging-market currencies are most active during local business hours, which may not match a trader’s usual screen time. Trading outside those periods can mean wider spreads or thinner liquidity, making execution less predictable even when the market appears calm.

Check spreads and trading availability at the times you plan to trade. Include a quiet period and relevant session overlaps, and note when key data or policy decisions are due. Rollover also matters: thinner liquidity and financing adjustments can affect prices and costs around that time. Confirm the broker’s trading hours and rollover arrangements for each instrument.

Why exotics often need wider stops and smaller size

A stop should account for a pair’s normal movement, current volatility and likely spread. If it is too close to ordinary price fluctuations, the position may close before the trade idea has room to develop. But widening a stop without changing position size increases the planned loss if the stop is reached.

Suppose a position with a 20-pip stop has a planned pip-based risk of 20 units, using the same pip value throughout. If the stop widens to 40 pips, halving the position size keeps the approximate pip-based risk at 20 units: 40 pips multiplied by half the size equals 20 pips at the original size. This illustrates the relationship; it is not a complete account-currency risk calculation.

For a real trade, convert pip value into account currency and include costs. For a pair quoted in the account currency, a basic conversion is: pip value = pip size × units of base currency. When the quote currency differs from the account currency, convert that pip value using the relevant exchange rate. Brokers may specify pip values and contract sizes directly, so check the contract details rather than relying on a generic lot assumption.

Exotic pairs can add complexity because exchange rates and conversion values may change quickly. Gaps or slippage can also make the realized loss larger than the amount estimated from the stop distance. Size the position in account currency, allow for costs and potential slippage, and do not treat a stop price as a guaranteed execution price.

Compare brokers on the costs you will actually pay

Start a useful broker comparison with like-for-like observations. Record live bid and ask prices for the same pairs, at the same position size and times, across several sessions. Include a quiet period and a relevant market overlap. Note the spread rather than relying on a single snapshot or the best figure in an advertisement.

Then estimate costs for your expected holding period. Include spread, commission, overnight financing, currency-conversion charges and any minimum ticket cost. A short-term trade may be dominated by spread and commission, while a position held for multiple nights may also incur substantial financing charges. The comparison depends on the instrument, account terms and duration.

Check account mechanics before opening a position. Confirm contract size, minimum trade size, margin requirements, stop-distance rules and whether the account has negative-balance protection. Margin is not the same as a trade’s cash risk: a small margin requirement does not mean a small possible loss. Verify which legal entity provides the account and what regulatory protections apply to that entity and client type.

Finally, assess execution, not just spreads. A demo account or small position can help you evaluate fills and slippage in ordinary conditions; scheduled news can show how quotes and execution behave in faster markets. These tests cannot guarantee future execution. A low headline spread alone does not establish low all-in costs or reliable fills when liquidity changes.

Key takeaways

  • Pair categories are useful shorthand, not a substitute for checking an individual instrument’s liquidity, spread and volatility.
  • Compare brokers using all-in costs at the times you plan to trade, not headline minimum spreads alone.
  • For exotics, size positions to account for wider stops, pip-value conversion and possible slippage or gaps.
  • Match session timing and event exposure to your strategy, and verify contract and margin terms before trading.

Frequently asked questions

Are major pairs always cheaper to trade?

Often, but not always. Spreads and execution costs depend on the broker, time of day, market conditions and whether commission is charged. Compare the total cost for your intended position size and holding period rather than assuming a major is the cheapest option.

Are crosses less volatile than majors?

There is no fixed rule. Volatility varies among individual pairs and can change after economic releases or policy surprises. Compare specific pairs using the same volatility measure and period, and account for the sessions and events that affect them.

Why use an exotic pair if its costs are higher?

An exotic may provide exposure to a market or currency view unavailable through a major pair. That exposure may come with wider costs, less predictable liquidity, gaps and more complicated position sizing. Make sure those risks and costs fit your trading plan before opening a position.

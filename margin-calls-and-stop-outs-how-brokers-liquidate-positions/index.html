Margin Calls and Stop-Outs: How Brokers Liquidate Positions

November 2, 2026 · Markets Notebook

A falling account balance can push a leveraged position toward forced closure sooner than expected, especially when several trades share margin. Knowing how a broker calculates equity, sets its triggers and chooses positions to close helps you assess that risk before placing an order.

Calculate margin level from equity and used margin

Margin level = equity ÷ used margin × 100. Equity is the account balance adjusted for the floating profit or loss on open positions. With $2,000 in equity and $1,000 in used margin, the margin level is 200%. As open trades lose money, equity falls and the margin level declines, even if the balance does not change.

Used margin is the collateral reserved to support open positions. It is not a trading fee or a guaranteed limit on losses: depending on the instrument, market movement and account protections, losses can exceed the margin initially required. Margin requirements can also change while a position is open.

Check how the broker defines equity. The treatment of credit, bonuses, commissions, swaps and other account-specific adjustments can affect the figure used to calculate margin level. Platform figures may update as prices change, but the client agreement and account disclosures set out the broker’s rules.

Separate a margin call warning from a stop-out trigger

A margin call is typically a warning or restriction, such as a notice that the account has fallen below a specified margin level. A stop-out is the threshold at which the broker may begin closing positions. The labels alone do not establish what the broker will do: check the account terms for the precise trigger and permitted actions.

Illustrative settings include a 100% margin call with a 50% stop-out, or a 50% margin call with a 20% stop-out. At a 50% stop-out, an account with $1,000 in used margin reaches the threshold when equity falls to $500. That does not mean every broker will liquidate at precisely that point in every market condition; execution and the broker’s stated procedure matter.

Illustrative trigger What it may indicate
100% margin call Warning or restriction when equity equals used margin
50% stop-out Position closures may begin when equity is half of used margin
20% stop-out Position closures may begin when equity is one-fifth of used margin

Liquidation procedures differ. Some brokers close positions in sequence, often starting with the largest losing position; others may use a different stated method. Closing a position changes used margin and equity, which can affect whether the broker closes another.

Work through a forex position using lots and leverage

For a USD-denominated account, one standard lot of EUR/USD represents 100,000 euros. With leverage of 1:100, illustrative required margin is about $1,000 when EUR/USD is near 1.00: the position’s approximate $100,000 notional value is divided by 100. This is an estimate, not a universal margin quote.

Two standard lots under the same assumptions represent about $200,000 and require roughly $2,000 in margin. With account equity of $3,000, the margin level is $3,000 ÷ $2,000 × 100, or 150%. If floating losses reduce equity to $1,000, the margin level falls to 50%, potentially reaching a broker’s 50% stop-out threshold.

Actual margin can differ based on the instrument price, account currency, contract size and leverage rules. Brokers may also set higher requirements for particular instruments, positions held around events or markets with reduced liquidity. Check the platform’s margin estimate and the applicable instrument specifications before entry.

Compare broker thresholds and liquidation rules

Record both the margin-call and stop-out percentages when comparing accounts. A warning level alone does not tell you when the broker may start closing trades. For example, two accounts may both show a 100% margin call but have stop-outs at 50% and 20%; the second allows equity to fall further relative to used margin before its stated liquidation trigger.

Confirm whether thresholds differ by account type, instrument, jurisdiction or market conditions. Check whether the broker can raise margin requirements, how it communicates changes and whether a new requirement applies to existing positions. A higher margin requirement increases used margin and can lower margin level, even without a new trade or a change in floating profit or loss.

Read the liquidation procedure as carefully as the percentages. Establish whether the broker may close all positions at once or closes them sequentially, and how it selects the first position. The independent breakdown of margin call and stop-out mechanics can help frame questions when comparing broker disclosures, but the broker’s current client agreement and platform terms determine the rules for a particular account.

Size positions to preserve a margin buffer

Estimate used margin before entry, then test how equity would change after plausible adverse price moves. Don’t rely only on the opening margin level: a position can start with a seemingly comfortable buffer and approach stop-out after a sharp move, an added trade or a change in margin requirements. Consider the combined exposure of all open positions, not just the trade being evaluated.

For example, with $5,000 of equity and $2,000 of used margin, the starting margin level is 250%. If the broker’s stop-out is 50% and used margin stays at $2,000, the threshold corresponds to $1,000 of equity. That leaves $4,000 of equity decline before the threshold in this simplified calculation. In practice, floating losses, fees and changing margin requirements can alter both figures.

If a realistic adverse move would bring margin level close to the broker’s stop-out, reduce lot size or keep more cash in the account. A stop-loss can help define an intended exit, but it does not guarantee protection against liquidation: gaps, slippage or fast markets can affect whether and at what price an order executes. A broker may also act under its stop-out terms before the stop-loss is filled.

Avoid common assumptions that distort the risk

Higher leverage lowers the required margin for a given position, but it does not reduce that position’s market exposure or potential loss. If a trader keeps the same lot size, a given price move generally produces the same trading loss regardless of the margin required to open the trade. Lower required margin can make it easier to take a larger position, increasing total exposure.

Unrealized losses reduce equity and margin level before a trade is closed. A margin call does not necessarily mean the broker will wait for a deposit before acting; restrictions or liquidation may follow under the account terms. Don’t treat a warning as extra time unless the broker’s rules explicitly provide it.

Recalculate after adding trades, withdrawing funds or holding positions through changes in margin requirements. Also account for costs such as spreads, commissions and overnight financing where relevant: they can reduce equity or affect the price at which a position reaches a risk threshold. Review the live account figures, not just the margin estimate made when the order was placed.

Key takeaways

  • Margin level equals equity divided by used margin, multiplied by 100.
  • Margin-call and stop-out percentages are separate triggers; compare both and read how liquidation is carried out.
  • Leverage affects required margin, not the underlying exposure or potential loss.
  • Size positions against plausible adverse moves and leave a buffer above the broker’s stop-out level.

Frequently asked questions

Does a margin call always mean my positions will be closed?

No. It may be a warning or restriction, but the broker’s stop-out rules determine when forced liquidation can begin. Check the account terms to see whether the broker can close positions at the margin-call level or only at a separate stop-out threshold.

Can a broker change its stop-out threshold?

Thresholds can vary by instrument, account or market conditions; check the current client agreement and platform disclosures. Confirm how the broker notifies clients of changes and whether revised margin requirements can apply to open positions.

Does using more leverage make a position safer from a stop-out?

No. It may reduce required margin, but the same position size has the same market exposure and can still generate the same loss. The margin level may look higher at entry because less margin is used, but an adverse move still reduces equity.

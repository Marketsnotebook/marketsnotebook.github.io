What Happens to Your Forex Funds When a Broker Goes Broke

November 13, 2026 · Markets Notebook

When a forex broker collapses, the fate of your deposited capital depends on how that money was held, not on the size of your trading account. Traders who assume their funds are “safe” simply because a broker advertises high leverage or low spreads often discover, after the fact, that the legal ownership of their cash is unclear. The following guide explains the mechanisms that protect—or expose—client money, and it offers a practical checklist for screening brokers before you trade.

Why Segregated Accounts Matter

Regulators in most major jurisdictions require brokers to keep client money separate from the cash they use to pay staff, rent offices, or finance their own trading activities. This separation is usually achieved by maintaining a ring-fenced bank account dedicated solely to client deposits. This is a legal condition of the licence, not merely a recommendation. For example, the UK Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) mandates that 100 % of client funds be held in a segregated account at a reputable, top-tier bank.

Segregation reduces the risk that a broker’s operating losses will diminish the pool of money belonging to traders. If the broker becomes insolvent, the bank holding the segregated account is typically an independent legal entity. A court can order the release of those funds to the clients without waiting for the broker’s other assets to be liquidated. In practice, this means that a trader’s capital is not part of the broker’s estate and therefore is not available to satisfy the broker’s creditors.

Because this requirement is uniform across regulated markets, a simple way to compare brokers is to look at independent resources such as proforexbrokers.com, which list the segregation status of dozens of firms. When a broker claims “all funds are safe,” verify that the claim is backed by a documented, audited segregated account rather than a vague internal policy.

Compensation Schemes vs. True Segregation

Many regulators operate a compensation fund that steps in when a broker fails and client money cannot be recovered. In the United Kingdom, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) covers up to £85,000 per person per firm; in some EU states, the limit is roughly €20,000. These schemes are useful as a safety net, but they are not a substitute for segregation.

  • Timing: A compensation claim can take months, even years, to process. During this time, a trader has no access to the funds held by the broker.
  • Coverage limits: The caps are modest compared with typical retail forex accounts, which often range from $5,000 to $100,000 or more. A trader with a $50,000 balance would receive only a fraction of that amount from the scheme.
  • Exclusions: “Excess insurance” policies that brokers sell to clients often contain exclusions for losses arising from market volatility, leverage misuse, or breaches of anti-money-laundering rules. The fine print can limit payouts significantly.

By contrast, a properly segregated account gives the trader immediate ownership of the cash. If the broker is liquidated, the segregated funds are identified, locked, and returned to the clients as part of the winding-up process. There is no need to wait for a compensation fund to approve a claim, and no arbitrary ceiling on the amount that can be reclaimed.

FXCM (2017)

When the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission ordered FXCM to cease operations, the broker’s assets were liquidated. Because the firm kept client deposits in segregated accounts at a major U.S. bank, roughly half of the outstanding balances were returned to traders after liquidation. The remaining shortfall was covered partially by the CFTC’s limited compensation fund, which capped payouts at $20,000 per customer.

Alpari (2015)

Alpari’s Russian arm collapsed after a severe devaluation of the ruble and a surge in margin calls. The company had placed client funds in a non-segregated account that was effectively part of its operating cash. When the firm entered bankruptcy, there were no separate assets to draw on, and traders lost the full amount of their deposits. This case underscores why the presence of a segregated account is more important than the size of the broker’s balance sheet.

Common Mistake

Both incidents involved brokers that marketed high leverage (e.g., 1:500) as a key selling point. Traders attracted by the promise of amplifying small deposits often overlooked custody arrangements, assuming that regulatory licensing alone guaranteed safety. In reality, the legal status of the money—segregated or pooled—determines whether a trader can recover funds quickly and fully.

How to Verify Segregation Before You Sign Up

  1. Request the segregation policy – Brokers that comply with regulatory requirements typically publish a “Client Money” page. The document should state the bank’s name, the account number (or at least the bank’s SWIFT code), and confirm that 100 % of client deposits are held in a ring-fenced account.
  2. Check the regulator’s register – Many supervisory bodies (e.g., FCA, ASIC, CySEC) maintain an online register indicating whether a firm’s client money is segregated. The entry may link to a PDF of the broker’s client money rules.
  3. Look for independent audits – A quarterly or annual audit by a Tier-1 accounting firm (e.g., PwC, Deloitte) that includes a “Bank Confirmation” section is a strong signal that the segregation claim is genuine. The audit should be publicly available or supplied on request.
  4. Confirm the bank’s credibility – Reputable banks are classified as “top-tier” by the regulator’s own list. Treat a claim citing a small, offshore bank with limited supervision with caution.

If any of these steps reveal vague language, missing documentation, or a reliance on “internal controls” rather than an external bank account, the broker’s segregation arrangement may be insufficient.

Offshore Gaps and What They Mean for Traders

Jurisdictions marketing themselves as “tax-friendly” or “low-cost”—such as Seychelles, Belize, or the Marshall Islands—often have no mandatory segregation rule. The local financial authority may require a broker to hold client money in a local bank, but that bank may not be legally required to keep the funds separate from the broker’s own assets.

Compensation schemes are also rare in offshore jurisdictions. Unlike the FCA or ASIC, many of these regulators do not operate a fund that compensates retail traders when a broker collapses. Consequently, the only protection is the broker’s own promise to keep client money safe. In practice, that promise is a contractual term, enforceable only through civil litigation in a foreign court, which can be costly and time-consuming.

For traders using an offshore broker, the prudent approach is to treat the segregation claim as a contractual representation rather than a regulator-backed right. This means verifying the bank’s reputation, demanding audited statements, and limiting exposure until the broker’s operational history is proven.

Action Checklist for Safe Broker Selection

  • ✅ Confirm 100 % segregation – Verify that client deposits are held in a dedicated account at a top-tier bank, and obtain the bank’s name and SWIFT code.
  • ✅ Regulatory license with compensation fund – Prefer brokers licensed by authorities that maintain a compensation scheme (e.g., FCA, ASIC, CNMV).
  • ✅ Review audited financial statements – Look for the most recent audit covering at least the past 12 months; check the “Client Money” note.
  • ✅ Test the withdrawal process – Initiate a small withdrawal (e.g., $100) and measure the time taken and any unexpected fees.

If any of these items cannot be satisfied, consider an alternative broker or limit the capital allocated until the missing pieces are clarified.

Key Takeaways

  • Segregated accounts grant direct ownership of your money; compensation schemes are a safety net, not primary protection.
  • Always verify the segregation arrangement with a reputable bank and obtain independent audit evidence.
  • Offshore brokers often lack regulatory safeguards – treat their segregation promises as contractual, not legal, guarantees.

Frequently Asked Questions

If my broker becomes insolvent, can I access my segregated funds instantly?

Generally yes – the segregation bank is a separate legal entity, so funds are usually returned to clients as part of the liquidation process without waiting for a compensation claim.

Do all brokers outside the EU have to segregate client money?

No. Many offshore regulators do not mandate segregation, so traders must rely on the broker’s own policies and audits.

What’s the difference between ‘client trust account’ and ‘segregated account’?

A trust account is a legal arrangement that holds client money separately, while a segregated account is the practical implementation – an actual bank account where the funds are ring-fenced.
